How a product
is actually built

Most product lifecycle diagrams give you a tidy circle with arrows and pretend that's enough. This one walks you through every stage, and at each step you'll watch a single example app, called Sprout, evolve from a half-formed hunch into something real.

🌱
Meet Sprout. A plant care companion app. Right now it's just an idea in someone's head. By the time you reach the bottom of this page, it'll be a living product with users, reviews, and a roadmap.
STAGE 01

Discovery & ideation

"What problem is worth solving, and for whom?"

Every product starts as a hunch. Someone notices a pattern of frustration, a workaround people keep building, or a gap in how things currently work. The job at this stage isn't to design a solution, it's to understand the problem deeply enough that you'd recognize a good solution if you saw one.

You're not looking for ideas. You're looking for pain. Real, specific, recurring pain that a real, specific group of people experiences.

Typical activities
user interviews problem framing jobs-to-be-done "why" questioning

STAGE 02

Research & validation

"Is this real, big enough, and not already solved?"

A pain point you noticed in three friends isn't yet a market. At this stage you pressure-test the hunch: how many people have it, how badly do they feel it, and what are they currently doing about it? You also look at what already exists and figure out why those solutions aren't working well enough.

The output here is a confident yes, no, or "yes but only if we approach it differently." Many ideas die at this stage. That's a feature, not a bug. Better to kill it on a slide than after six months of building.

STAGE 03

Define the MVP

"What is the smallest thing we could ship that actually proves the idea?"

MVP stands for minimum viable product. The trap most teams fall into is reading "minimum" as "small list of features" and then quietly making the list longer. The real test is sharper: if we ship just this, would it solve the core problem for someone, even imperfectly?

You're drawing a hard line between what gets built first, what gets built later, and what gets cut entirely. Everything that survives the line has to earn its place by being essential to the central promise of the product.

prioritization scoping roadmapping user stories

STAGE 04

Design the experience

"How will this actually feel to use?"

Design happens in layers. You start with rough wireframes (boxes and arrows, no styling) to figure out the bones of each screen and how someone moves through them. Then you layer in visual design, brand, copy, and interaction details to make it feel like a place rather than a form.

The best teams prototype early and test on real people before writing a line of production code. A confused user staring at your Figma file is worth ten polished opinions from inside the team.

Typical activities
wireframing user flows prototyping usability tests

STAGE 05

Build it

"How do we go from designs to working software?"

Engineering takes the designs and the requirements and turns them into something real. The work usually breaks into frontend (what the user sees and touches), backend (the data, logic, and integrations), and infrastructure (where it runs, how it scales, how it stays up).

Good teams ship in small, frequent increments rather than disappearing for six months and reemerging with a tablet from the mountain. Each merged change brings the product a measurable step closer to the MVP.

STAGE 06

Test & refine

"Does it actually work, and does it work for real people?"

Testing is not one thing, it's a stack of overlapping checks. Unit tests verify that individual functions behave correctly. Integration tests confirm that pieces play nicely together. QA catches the things humans notice that machines don't. Beta users reveal the gap between how you think people will use it and how they actually do.

Every bug found at this stage is a bug your launch users don't get to find for you. This is also where security review, accessibility checks, and performance benchmarking live.

Typical activities
automated tests QA cycles beta testing bug triage

STAGE 07

Launch into the world

"How do we get this in front of the people who need it?"

Launching is more than pressing deploy. There's the technical release (app store submission, rollout strategy, monitoring dashboards), and the go-to-market motion (positioning, messaging, channels, support readiness). A launch with no audience is a tree falling in an empty forest.

Smart teams launch in waves: a soft launch to a small region or a closed beta cohort first, then a wider release once they've watched the metrics and patched the obvious cracks. Big bang launches make great press releases and brittle products.

store submission marketing support setup monitoring

STAGE 08

Iterate & grow

"What does the data say, and what do we build next?"

Launch isn't the finish line, it's the moment your assumptions meet reality. Now you have actual users generating actual signals: where they get stuck, what they ignore, what they ask for, where they churn. Your job is to listen carefully and choose what deserves a response.

Iteration runs on a feedback loop: measure, learn, prioritize, ship, measure again. The features you cut in Stage 3 come back into the conversation now, but only the ones the data actually argues for. The product is no longer just yours, it belongs to its users too.

Typical activities
analytics review user research feature releases retention work

It isn't a line. It's a loop.

The numbered stages are a useful teaching tool, but in practice a healthy product cycles through them continuously. Every feature is its own small lifecycle, riding on top of the larger one.

A product is never really finished. It just keeps becoming a better version of itself, in conversation with the people who use it.
