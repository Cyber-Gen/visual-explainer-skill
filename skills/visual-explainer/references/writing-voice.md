# Writing Voice and Content Guide

The visual design carries half the artifact. The prose carries the other half. This is how to write the prose so it pulls its weight.

## The voice in one paragraph

Confident, warm, lightly editorial. Like a smart older friend who happens to know this domain and is taking the time to explain it well to you over coffee. Not lecturing. Not breezy. Not jargon-heavy. Specific where it matters, restrained where it doesn't. Occasionally clever, never showing off.

## Structural rules

### Two short paragraphs per stage

Each stage's explanation is exactly two paragraphs, ~50-65 words each, ~100-130 words total. Three paragraphs feels bloated. One paragraph feels thin. Two creates the right rhythm.

**Paragraph one**: define the concept clearly and concretely. Avoid abstraction in the opening sentence. Open with what's actually happening.

**Paragraph two**: add the nuance, the common mistake, the surprising fact, or the principle behind the principle. This is where the artifact earns the reader's respect.

### Bold the key terms

Within prose, bold 1-3 key terms per paragraph. These should be the words the reader will want to highlight if reading on paper. The terms should be the ones that name the thing being taught, not just emphatic words.

Good bolding: "**MVP** stands for minimum viable product."
Bad bolding: "This stage is **really important** to get right."

### Use specifics over generics

Generic phrasing makes the artifact feel like filler. Specifics make it feel like someone actually thought about this. Numbers, named examples, concrete scenarios all help.

Bad: "Many teams skip this step and it causes problems later."
Better: "Many teams skip this step and it costs them three to six months of rebuild later."
Best: "Skip this step and you'll learn what every team before you has learned: it costs three to six months of rebuild work that the design phase would have saved you."

### Avoid hedge fog

A sentence that contains "may," "might," "could," "potentially," "often," "typically," and "tends to" all at once has said nothing. Pick one hedge per sentence at most, and only when accuracy actually demands it.

## The guiding question

Every stage opens with a single italic question. This is the highest-leverage sentence in the whole artifact — it tells the reader why they care about this stage. Spend real time on it.

Rules for guiding questions:

- One sentence, ends with a question mark
- 6 to 15 words
- Phrased as the question the practitioner is asking themselves at this moment
- Specific enough to be answerable, broad enough to cover the whole stage

Good: *"What is the smallest thing we could ship that proves the idea?"*
Bad: *"How do we define MVP?"* (too generic, asks about the term not the thing)

## Headings

Format: `Stage title <em>with italicized word</em>`

The italicized word should be the one carrying conceptual weight. Italicizing the right word makes the heading scan as a phrase, not just a label.

Good: `Discovery <em>& ideation</em>` (the italicization links the two halves)
Good: `Define <em>the MVP</em>` (italicizes the noun being defined)
Bad: `<em>Discovery</em> & ideation` (italicizes the topic word, looks emphatic for no reason)

## The closing synthesis

The closing section is short but load-bearing. Three components:

1. **The reframe**: one sentence that recontextualizes everything just covered. "It isn't a line. It's a loop." or "What looked like eight stages is really one habit, repeated."
2. **The supporting paragraph**: 60-90 words elaborating on the reframe. Why is this the truer picture? What does it mean for the reader's practice?
3. **Optional: the exercise**: 1-2 sentences inviting the reader to apply the framework to their own life. "Now pick an X you actually use and try to imagine its journey." Active recall cements the learning.

## Hero intro

The hero sits above the first stage and orients the reader. It has three sentences worth of work to do:

1. Name what the artifact will do
2. Acknowledge what most explainers of this topic get wrong (or what makes this one different)
3. Introduce the threading example by name

Then a small companion card (smaller box) shows the threading example in its starting state.

## Activity tags

The small mono-font tags after each stage's explanation. 3-5 per stage. They name the actual activities a practitioner does at this stage — verbs as gerunds or short noun phrases.

Good: `user interviews`, `competitor analysis`, `bug triage`, `code review`
Bad: `understanding the problem`, `being thoughtful`, `taking action`

These should read like the chapter labels of a textbook, not motivational phrases.

## Common voice mistakes

**Sounding like a project manager**: "Stakeholders must align on requirements before proceeding to the next phase." Translate into human English: "Get the people who care about this on the same page before you keep going."

**Sounding like a textbook**: "It is important to note that the process of validation involves several key components." Just write the components.

**Sounding like a LinkedIn post**: "Here's a 🔥 truth most founders won't tell you about MVPs..." Don't.

**Sounding like a robot**: "This stage encompasses the activities of design and prototyping." Use the active voice. "At this stage you design and prototype."

**Over-qualifying everything**: "Some teams may, depending on context, sometimes consider it useful to occasionally..." Pick a stance. The reader can disagree with a strong claim. They can't engage with a fog.

## Tone modulation by topic

The default voice is warm-editorial, but tilt it for the topic:

- **Technical/engineering**: drop slightly into precision and dry humor. Less warmth, more specificity.
- **Creative/artistic**: lean into the writerly side. Allow more metaphor.
- **Business/strategic**: stay direct and decision-oriented. Less metaphor, more concrete consequences.
- **Personal/emotional**: warmer, more second-person, more permission-giving language.

The aesthetic palette (warm editorial vs cool technical vs refined minimal) should match the voice tilt. They're not independent decisions.

## Avoiding the AI tell

Specific patterns that make prose feel AI-generated and should be cut:

- "Let's dive in" / "Let's explore" / "Let's unpack"
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "It's important to note that..."
- "At its core..."
- "Whether you're a beginner or a seasoned expert..."
- Paragraphs that end with a rhetorical question summary
- Every paragraph starting with a transitional connective ("Furthermore," "Moreover," "Additionally,")
- Three-part lists that come out of nowhere ("clarity, confidence, and conviction")

If you catch yourself writing any of these, rewrite. They are tells.
